random-walk-is-binomial

Fri Jul 03 2026

The Problem

A particle starts at 0 on the number line and takes nnn steps. Each step is +1+1+1 or −1-1−1 with equal probability. After nnn steps, what is the distribution of its position?

I worked it out by hand for the first few nnn. Some things jumped out of those PX1,PX2,PX3P_{X_1}, P_{X_2}, P_{X_3}PX1​​,PX2​​,PX3​​ tables:

  • After 1 step it sits on ±1\pm 1±1. After 2 steps, only even positions. After 3 steps, only odd positions. The reachable set flips parity with nnn.
  • Each distribution is symmetric about 0, with the most mass near 0 and less toward the edges.
  • It looked like a bell, or like the triangle you get from summing two dice, which peaks at 7.

What I could not do was jump to general nnn. Redoing the tree for every nnn is hopeless. I needed the position's PMF as a formula, and I did not see how to get there.

Where I Got Stuck

The position after nnn steps is a sum of nnn random ±1\pm 1±1 steps:

Y=X1+X2+⋯+Xn,Xi∈{−1,+1}.Y = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n, \qquad X_i \in \{-1, +1\}.Y=X1​+X2​+⋯+Xn​,Xi​∈{−1,+1}.

Working directly with a sum of fluctuating +1+1+1s and −1-1−1s is what blocked me. Each new step splits every position into two, and after nnn steps the bookkeeping explodes. I had no handle on P(Y=y)P(Y = y)P(Y=y) for a general nnn and yyy.

The Reframe: Count Rights, Not the Running Sum

The move that unlocked it: stop tracking the running sum, and count the two kinds of step instead. Let RRR be the number of right steps and LLL the number of left steps. Two facts then pin down everything:

n=R+L,Y=R−L.n = R + L, \qquad Y = R - L.n=R+L,Y=R−L.

The first says every step is either a right or a left. The second is the position, since each right adds 1 and each left subtracts 1.

Now RRR is easy to name. Over nnn independent steps, each going right with probability 1/21/21/2, RRR is a count of successes in nnn fair trials. So R∼Bin(n,1/2)R \sim \text{Bin}(n, 1/2)R∼Bin(n,1/2) and

P(R=r)=(nr)(12)r(12)n−r=(nr)12n.P(R = r) = \binom{n}{r} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{r} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n-r} = \binom{n}{r} \frac{1}{2^{n}}.P(R=r)=(rn​)(21​)r(21​)n−r=(rn​)2n1​.

Both probabilities are 1/21/21/2, so pr(1−p)n−rp^{r}(1-p)^{n-r}pr(1−p)n−r collapses to 1/2n1/2^{n}1/2n no matter what rrr is. The entire shape of the distribution is carried by the count (nr)\binom{n}{r}(rn​) alone.

From R to the Position y

I still had the probability in terms of RRR, not the position yyy I actually care about. The same two equations bridge them. Adding n=R+Ln = R + Ln=R+L and Y=R−LY = R - LY=R−L drops LLL and gives R=n+y2R = \frac{n + y}{2}R=2n+y​. Landing at position yyy forces exactly n+y2\frac{n+y}{2}2n+y​ of the steps to have been rights. Substitute:

P(Y=y)=(nn+y2)12n.P(Y = y) = \binom{n}{\frac{n+y}{2}} \frac{1}{2^{n}}.P(Y=y)=(2n+y​n​)2n1​.

That is the whole PMF. Two constraints fix its domain, and they explain the parity pattern I had noticed by hand:

  • Parity. The count n+y2\frac{n+y}{2}2n+y​ has to be a whole number, so n+yn + yn+y must be even, so yyy shares the parity of nnn. Even nnn lands on even positions, odd nnn on odd positions, and off-parity positions get probability 0.
  • Range. The number of rights runs 0≤n+y2≤n0 \le \frac{n+y}{2} \le n0≤2n+y​≤n, which is just −n≤y≤n-n \le y \le n−n≤y≤n. You cannot get further from 0 than the number of steps you took.

The textbook runs the same derivation in its random walk example (Example 3.7.2 in the 2nd edition), writing the position as Y=2X−nY = 2X - nY=2X−n with X∼Bin(n,1/2)X \sim \text{Bin}(n, 1/2)X∼Bin(n,1/2) counting the right steps. That is the same R−LR - LR−L in different letters, since X=RX = RX=R and n−X=Ln - X = Ln−X=L.

Quick check against my hand tables: for n=3n = 3n=3, P(Y=1)=(32)18=38P(Y = 1) = \binom{3}{2}\frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8}P(Y=1)=(23​)81​=83​, exactly the 3/83/83/8 I had written for position 1 under PX3P_{X_3}PX3​​.

The Click: One Shape, Three Disguises

Once the PMF is (n(n+y)/2)/2n\binom{n}{(n+y)/2} / 2^{n}((n+y)/2n​)/2n, the bell stops being a surprise. The 1/2n1/2^{n}1/2n is a constant. The whole shape is the binomial coefficient (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) swept across kkk, and I already knew that coefficient peaks in the middle and drops off toward the ends.

Why the coefficient peaks

Two arguments, and the second is the one worth keeping.

Symmetry rules out a monotonic slide. Choosing kkk items to take is the same as choosing the n−kn - kn−k to leave behind, so (nk)=(nn−k)\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}(kn​)=(n−kn​). The values at kkk and n−kn-kn−k match, which forces a symmetric hump rather than a curve that just keeps falling.

The ratio test says where the hump turns over. Compare one coefficient to the next:

(nk+1)(nk)=n−kk+1.\frac{\binom{n}{k+1}}{\binom{n}{k}} = \frac{n-k}{k+1}.(kn​)(k+1n​)​=k+1n−k​.

While this ratio is above 1, the sequence is still climbing. Setting n−kk+1≥1\frac{n-k}{k+1} \ge 1k+1n−k​≥1 rearranges to n−k≥k+1n - k \ge k + 1n−k≥k+1, that is k≤n−12k \le \frac{n-1}{2}k≤2n−1​. So the coefficients grow up to about the middle and shrink after it. For even nnn there is a single peak at k=n/2k = n/2k=n/2. For odd nnn the ratio equals exactly 1 at the center, so the two middle values tie.

The same shape everywhere

Map that back to the walk. The count of rights is k=n+y2k = \frac{n+y}{2}k=2n+y​, and (nk)\binom{n}{k}(kn​) is largest at k=n/2k = n/2k=n/2. Setting n+y2=n2\frac{n+y}{2} = \frac{n}{2}2n+y​=2n​ gives y=0y = 0y=0. The most likely place to end up is where you started, with the probability decaying symmetrically toward ±n\pm n±n. That is the same peak, relabeled from "number of rights kkk" to "position yyy".

It is also the same shape as summing two dice. There, middle sums like 7 have many more combinations (1+6,2+5,3+4,…1{+}6, 2{+}5, 3{+}4, \dots1+6,2+5,3+4,…) than edge sums like 2, which only comes from 1+11{+}11+1, so mass piles up in the center.

Worth pinning down what that dice sum actually is. With XXX and YYY the two dice, X+YX + YX+Y is not a Binomial. A die is uniform on {1,…,6}\{1, \dots, 6\}{1,…,6}, not a Bernoulli, so the sum is a triangular distribution, the convolution of two uniforms, which is why its plot has straight sides rather than a smooth hump. It shares the Binomial's shape through the mechanism, not the formula: both are sums of independent identical pieces, and summing independent pieces piles mass in the middle, since more input pairs land on the central values. The walk lands on the Binomial exactly because its pieces are ±1\pm 1±1 Bernoulli steps, while the dice sum lands on a triangle because its pieces are uniform. Same family, different members.

Binomial coefficients, dice sums, and the random walk are the same peaked, symmetric object wearing different labels. The random-walk PMF is a relabeled Bin(n,1/2)\text{Bin}(n, 1/2)Bin(n,1/2), and nothing more. The reason it keeps smoothing toward a cleaner bell as nnn grows has a name, the Central Limit Theorem: a sum of many independent identical pieces tends toward a bell whatever the pieces look like, and the walk is exactly such a sum.

Aside: y on Both Sides Is Fine

For a while the formula bothered me because yyy shows up on the left and the right of P(Y=y)=…P(Y = y) = \dotsP(Y=y)=…. That looked like an equation I should solve for yyy. It is not. P(Y=y)P(Y = y)P(Y=y) is a function definition, read as "the probability that the random variable YYY takes the specific value yyy". Capital YYY is the random variable, lowercase yyy is a particular number you plug in, and the PMF is often written pY(y)p_Y(y)pY​(y) to make that explicit. It is no stranger than f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2f(x)=x2 having xxx on both sides. That capital-vs-lowercase split is exactly the machinery behind probability-distributions-and-random-variables.
