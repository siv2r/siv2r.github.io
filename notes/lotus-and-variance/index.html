LOTUS: the shortcut that is secretly a theorem

To compute E(X2)E(X^2)E(X2) for a die roll, the definition of expectation says I owe the PMF of X2X^2X2 first: values of X2X^2X2, weighted by probabilities of X2X^2X2. But everyone just writes (1+4+9+16+25+36)/6=91/6(1+4+9+16+25+36)/6 = 91/6(1+4+9+16+25+36)/6=91/6 and never finds that PMF. Why is that legal?

For X2X^2X2 the question barely bites. Squaring is one-to-one on {1,…,6}\{1,\dots,6\}{1,…,6}, so the six weights carry over unchanged and the formula is the definition with values relabeled. The real test is a function with collisions:

W=(X−3.5)2W = (X - 3.5)^2W=(X−3.5)2

Now x=1x=1x=1 and x=6x=6x=6 both map to 6.256.256.25, x=2x=2x=2 and 555 to 2.252.252.25, x=3x=3x=3 and 444 to 0.250.250.25. The relabeling defense is dead. Compute both ways:

  • Honest route, PMF of WWW first: E(W)=13(0.25)+13(2.25)+13(6.25)=35/12≈2.92E(W) = \tfrac13(0.25) + \tfrac13(2.25) + \tfrac13(6.25) = 35/12 \approx 2.92E(W)=31​(0.25)+31​(2.25)+31​(6.25)=35/12≈2.92.
  • Shortcut, never forming WWW's PMF: ∑x(x−3.5)2⋅16\sum_x (x-3.5)^2 \cdot \tfrac16∑x​(x−3.5)2⋅61​ over the six faces.

Pair the shortcut's six terms by collision and each brace holds a constant value of WWW with weight 16+16=13\tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac1361​+61​=31​, which is exactly P(W=w)P(W=w)P(W=w). The shortcut is the honest sum, rearranged. That regrouping is the entire proof, and it is the same move that proves linearity of expectation. The move itself lives in pebble-grouping.

The general statement is Theorem 4.5.1, the law of the unconscious statistician: for any function ggg,

E(g(X))=∑xg(x) P(X=x).E(g(X)) = \sum_x g(x)\,P(X=x).E(g(X))=∑x​g(x)P(X=x).

The name is a joke. You swap xxx for g(x)g(x)g(x) in the expectation formula without thinking, and the theorem's content is that the thoughtless substitution is correct. For one-to-one ggg it is pure relabeling. For many-to-one ggg, several P(X=x)P(X=x)P(X=x) values pool into one P(g(X)=w)P(g(X)=w)P(g(X)=w), like the collision pairs above. Rearranging is the word that covers both cases, relabeling only covers the first.

What LOTUS does not license

The tempting sibling shortcut, E(g(X))=g(E(X))E(g(X)) = g(E(X))E(g(X))=g(E(X)), is false for nonlinear ggg. The die already shows it: E(X2)=15.17E(X^2) = 15.17E(X2)=15.17 but (EX)2=12.25(EX)^2 = 12.25(EX)2=12.25. For linear ggg the two agree, and that is just linearity of expectation again.

St. Petersburg shows how badly it can fail. Flip a fair coin until the first heads. Let XXX be the number of flips, so P(X=n)=(1/2)nP(X=n) = (1/2)^nP(X=n)=(1/2)n and E(X)=2E(X) = 2E(X)=2 (waiting times average 1/p1/p1/p). The casino pays 2X2^X2X dollars. The naive shortcut prices the game at 2E(X)=42^{E(X)} = 42E(X)=4 dollars. LOTUS prices it honestly:

E(2X)=∑n≥12n⋅(1/2)n=1+1+1+⋯=∞.E(2^X) = \sum_{n \ge 1} 2^n \cdot (1/2)^n = 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty.E(2X)=∑n≥1​2n⋅(1/2)n=1+1+1+⋯=∞.

Every term is exactly 1 and the support never stops, since no flip count is guaranteed to end the game. So by the expectation criterion, no finite ticket price is too high. Pay a million, the game is still a bargain. My gut said a couple of dollars, and the gut is onto something real: the game almost always ends within a few flips and pays pocket change. The mean is infinite only because it is propped up by astronomically rare jackpots.

Both answers are right about different things. Expectation is a center of mass, not a typical value. It tells you where the distribution balances and nothing about how far a single play lands from that point. That gap is what variance exists to fill.

Variance: the second number

The naive spread measure fails first. Averaging the signed deviations gives E(X−EX)=EX−EX=0E(X - EX) = EX - EX = 0E(X−EX)=EX−EX=0, for every distribution, by linearity. This is not a symmetry accident. Take X=0X = 0X=0 with probability 0.90.90.9 and X=10X = 10X=10 with probability 0.10.10.1. The mean is 1, and the deviations are −1-1−1 carrying weight 0.90.90.9 against +9+9+9 carrying weight 0.10.10.1. They cancel. The mean is the point where probability-weighted pulls from left and right agree, so signed deviations always net to zero.

The fix is to kill the signs before averaging:

Var(X)=E[(X−EX)2].\mathrm{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - EX)^2\big].Var(X)=E[(X−EX)2].

For the die this is exactly the collision exercise: W=(X−3.5)2W = (X-3.5)^2W=(X−3.5)2, so Var(X)=35/12≈2.92\mathrm{Var}(X) = 35/12 \approx 2.92Var(X)=35/12≈2.92. I had computed the die's variance before knowing its name.

Why square instead of absolute value? E∣X−EX∣E|X - EX|E∣X−EX∣ is a legitimate spread measure (mean absolute deviation), so the square has to win on merit. Three reasons, in increasing order of importance:

  1. Algebra. (X−μ)2(X-\mu)^2(X−μ)2 expands into X2−2μX+μ2X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2X2−2μX+μ2 and the expansion feeds straight into linearity. Every clean variance formula flows from this. Absolute values do not expand and give almost no formulas.
  2. Additivity. For independent XXX and YYY, Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y). Spreads add. Mean absolute deviation obeys no such law. This is what gives binomial variance in one line below, and it is the engine behind n\sqrt{n}n​ behavior, Chebyshev, and the tail bounds crypto arguments lean on.
  3. Geometry. Squared deviations give independent contributions a Pythagorean structure. They combine like perpendicular vectors, and SD behaves like a Euclidean length. The additivity in point 2 is the Pythagorean theorem in disguise.

The standing complaint, wrong units (the die's 2.92 is in squared pips), is patched by SD(X)=Var(X)≈1.71\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)} \approx 1.71SD(X)=Var(X)​≈1.71 pips.

Expanding the definition with linearity gives the workhorse form:

Var(X)=E(X2)−(EX)2.\mathrm{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - (EX)^2.Var(X)=E(X2)−(EX)2.

The E(X2)E(X^2)E(X2) piece is a LOTUS computation, which closes the loop: variance is an E(g(X))E(g(X))E(g(X)), and LOTUS is the license to compute it from XXX's PMF alone. Die check: 91/6−3.52=15.1667−12.25=2.916791/6 - 3.5^2 = 15.1667 - 12.25 = 2.916791/6−3.52=15.1667−12.25=2.9167, the same 35/1235/1235/12 as the honest route. One notation trap the book itself flags: write (EX)2(EX)^2(EX)2, not EX2EX^2EX2. The two differ by exactly the variance.

What variance is asking

Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean, and it answers one question: if I draw once, how far from the center should I expect to land? It measures unpredictability, not size. A random variable that always outputs 42 is huge but perfectly predictable, so its variance is 0.

That is the whole intuition behind Var(c)=0\mathrm{Var}(c) = 0Var(c)=0. Spread is disagreement between draws. A constant never surprises: every draw lands exactly on the mean, the distance is 0 on every single draw, and the average of all zeros is 0. In the pebble picture, a constant piles every pebble on one spot, which then is the balance point, so no pebble sits any distance away. Zero variance means the distribution has collapsed to a point.

The same reading applied to the distributions I know:

  • Bernoulli(p): p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p), maximal at p=1/2p = 1/2p=1/2, the most unpredictable coin. As p→0p \to 0p→0 or p→1p \to 1p→1 the variance slides to 0 because the coin degenerates into a constant.
  • Binomial(n, p): np(1−p)np(1-p)np(1−p), the same per-flip uncertainty added over nnn independent copies.
  • Uniform(a, b): (b−a)2/12(b-a)^2/12(b−a)2/12. The spread depends only on how wide the room is, not where the room is. Shifting costs nothing, and squeezing the width to zero collapses it back to a constant.
  • Fair die: 35/12≈2.9235/12 \approx 2.9235/12≈2.92, six distinct outcomes each holding mass away from 3.5.

Indicators and binomial variance

An indicator III is 1 if an event happens (probability ppp) and 0 otherwise. One coin flip, one trial, one bit of a hash output. Indicators are the only random variables whose square is themselves: 02=00^2 = 002=0 and 12=11^2 = 112=1, so I2=II^2 = II2=I identically and E(I2)=E(I)=pE(I^2) = E(I) = pE(I2)=E(I)=p with no computation. Then

Var(I)=p−p2=p(1−p).\mathrm{Var}(I) = p - p^2 = p(1-p).Var(I)=p−p2=p(1−p).

The shape is a sanity check by itself. Variance is 0 at p=0p=0p=0 and p=1p=1p=1 (a certain event does not vary) and maximal at p=1/2p = 1/2p=1/2, value 1/41/41/4. An unbiased bit is the most unpredictable bit.

A binomial count X∼Bin(n,p)X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p)X∼Bin(n,p) is a sum of nnn independent indicators, so the additivity law gives Var(X)=np(1−p)\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)Var(X)=np(1−p) in one line. Compare where the assumptions land: E(X)=npE(X) = npE(X)=np never consulted independence, because linearity holds unconditionally (see pebble-grouping for why). Variance addition is the step that consumes it.

Variance is not linear, and I called it that I wrote "linearity of variance" during the session, and the book stamps a biohazard next to that idea (4.6.3). The failure case is Y=XY = XY=X itself: Var(X+X)=Var(2X)=4 Var(X)\mathrm{Var}(X+X) = \mathrm{Var}(2X) = 4\,\mathrm{Var}(X)Var(X+X)=Var(2X)=4Var(X), not 2 Var(X)2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)2Var(X). Dependence makes spread compound rather than add. The correct rules: constants escape squared, Var(cX)=c2 Var(X)\mathrm{Var}(cX) = c^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)Var(cX)=c2Var(X) (so SD, not variance, scales like the quantity itself). Shifts vanish, Var(X+c)=Var(X)\mathrm{Var}(X+c) = \mathrm{Var}(X)Var(X+c)=Var(X). And Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y)\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y) only under independence.
