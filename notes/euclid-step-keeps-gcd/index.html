euclid-step-keeps-gcd

Sun Sep 13 2026

The Euclidean algorithm replaces the pair (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) by (b,r)(b, r)(b,r), where a=qb+ra = q b + ra=qb+r and 0≤r<b0 \le r < b0≤r<b, and repeats until the remainder is 0. It computes gcd⁡(a,b)\gcd(a, b)gcd(a,b) only if every step preserves it:

gcd⁡(a,b)=gcd⁡(b,r).\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r).gcd(a,b)=gcd(b,r).

Where I got stuck

Picture aaa as a strip cut into qqq segments of length bbb and a leftover of length rrr. Let d=gcd⁡(a,b)d = \gcd(a, b)d=gcd(a,b), the biggest block that tiles both aaa and bbb. The block ddd tiles the whole strip and tiles each bbb segment, so it tiles the leftover:

r=a−qb=(k1−qk2) d.r = a - q b = (k_1 - q k_2)\, d .r=a−qb=(k1​−qk2​)d.

So ddd divides both bbb and rrr. I read that as "ddd is the gcd of (b,r)(b, r)(b,r) as well", equality, done. When the write-up said this only proves gcd⁡(a,b)≤gcd⁡(b,r)\gcd(a, b) \le \gcd(b, r)gcd(a,b)≤gcd(b,r), I pushed back for three turns. I had started with ddd being the gcd, not some factor, so where could an inequality come from?

What the argument actually used

One property of ddd: it divides aaa and bbb. It never used that ddd is the largest such number. So any block with that property survives the same argument, and the conclusion is only what is true of every such block: ddd is a common divisor of (b,r)(b, r)(b,r). Being the gcd of (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) is a fact about the pair (a,b)(a, b)(a,b). It says nothing about how the pair (b,r)(b, r)(b,r) ranks its divisors.

The counterexample is to the inference, not to the number. Take a=24a = 24a=24, b=18b = 18b=18, so 24=1⋅18+624 = 1 \cdot 18 + 624=1⋅18+6. The block 3 tiles 24 and 18, so by the same argument it tiles r=6r = 6r=6. All true, and gcd⁡(18,6)=6\gcd(18, 6) = 6gcd(18,6)=6, not 3. The argument's shape cannot tell 3 from 6, so it cannot conclude "greatest" for either.

What the strip argument proves, exactly: every common divisor of (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) is a common divisor of (b,r)(b, r)(b,r). Hence gcd⁡(a,b)≤gcd⁡(b,r)\gcd(a, b) \le \gcd(b, r)gcd(a,b)≤gcd(b,r), and nothing more.

The other direction

The way it closed: suppose (b,r)(b, r)(b,r) had a common divisor eee bigger than ddd. Then eee divides bbb and rrr, so it divides qb+r=aq b + r = aqb+r=a. That makes eee a common divisor of (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) bigger than the greatest one, which is impossible. So no common divisor of (b,r)(b, r)(b,r) exceeds ddd, and with the first half that is equality.

Written out directly it is the same line. Take any eee dividing both bbb and rrr, say b=em1b = e m_1b=em1​ and r=em2r = e m_2r=em2​. Then

a=qb+r=e (qm1+m2),a = q b + r = e\,(q m_1 + m_2),a=qb+r=e(qm1​+m2​),

so eee divides aaa too. Every common divisor of (b,r)(b, r)(b,r) is a common divisor of (a,b)(a, b)(a,b), which gives gcd⁡(b,r)≤gcd⁡(a,b)\gcd(b, r) \le \gcd(a, b)gcd(b,r)≤gcd(a,b).

The two directions use different halves of the same equation. The first subtracts, r=a−qbr = a - q br=a−qb, and the second adds back, a=qb+ra = q b + ra=qb+r. Each is one line, and neither alone is the lemma. On the counterexample pair the chain runs (24,18)→(18,6)→(6,0)(24, 18) \to (18, 6) \to (6, 0)(24,18)→(18,6)→(6,0) with gcd 6 at every stage. On a coprime pair, (12,5)→(5,2)→(2,1)→(1,0)(12, 5) \to (5, 2) \to (2, 1) \to (1, 0)(12,5)→(5,2)→(2,1)→(1,0), the gcd is 1 throughout, which is what makes the back-substitution in units-mod-n-coprime-and-totient land on 1.

The general lesson: an argument that uses only a shared property cannot single out the extreme element. To prove "ddd is the greatest" the word greatest has to enter somewhere, and here it enters only through the reverse direction. Same shape as one-to-one-proof-converse-trap: one direction proved, both directions claimed.

Mantra "Tiles both" makes it a factor. "Greatest" needs the other direction.
